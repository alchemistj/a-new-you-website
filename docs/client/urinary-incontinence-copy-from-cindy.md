You sneeze. You laugh. You pick up a grandbaby—and a little urine sneaks out.

Or you pull into the driveway and suddenly reaching the front door feels like an Olympic event.

If this sounds familiar, you are not alone.

*No embarrassment required.*

You don’t need to know the medical terms, identify the type of incontinence, or arrive with a treatment plan.

**Urinary incontinence means urine leaks when you don’t intend for it to.**

It may be an occasional small leak or frequent leakage that affects exercise, intimacy, travel, work, sleep, or confidence.

Incontinence is common, but it is not one condition with one universal solution.

Your symptoms, health history, medications, pelvic floor function, hormonal changes, and other factors all may matter.

Understanding the pattern can help us determine how to care for you, what treatments may be appropriate, and whether additional evaluation is needed.

## Understanding the Different Types of Bladder Leakage

Different types of urinary incontinence can feel similar, but their triggers and treatment approaches may differ.

Identifying the pattern is one reason an evaluation can be helpful.

### Stress Incontinence

Stress incontinence occurs when pressure on the bladder causes leakage.

Coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting, running, jumping, or other physical activity may trigger it.

Here, “stress” means physical pressure—not emotional stress.

It may occur when the pelvic floor or other support structures don’t provide enough support during these activities.

### Urge Incontinence

Urge incontinence occurs when a sudden, difficult-to-delay need to urinate is followed by leakage before you reach the bathroom.

You may feel you have to go immediately, even when your bladder is not very full.

Urgency without leakage can also disrupt daily life and may be worth discussing.

### Mixed Incontinence

Mixed incontinence includes both stress and urge leakage.

For example, you may leak when you sneeze and also have urgency that makes it difficult to reach the bathroom in time.

Treatment may need to address both patterns.

### Overflow Incontinence

Other patterns include overflow incontinence, which occurs when the bladder does not empty fully and becomes overly full.

Possible signs include dribbling, a weak stream, frequent urination, or feeling that you still need to go after using the bathroom.

### Functional Incontinence

Functional incontinence occurs when mobility limitations, cognitive changes, difficulty removing clothing, or trouble reaching the bathroom prevent timely toileting.

In this situation, the bladder may function normally, but another barrier makes getting there difficult.

These patterns can overlap, and symptoms don’t always fit neatly into one category. Each may require a different approach.

**One exercise, medication, device, or procedure cannot address every cause of urinary leakage.**

## Why It Happens

Your bladder, pelvic floor muscles, urethra, nerves, hormones, and surrounding tissues work together to store and release urine.

A change in any part of this system can affect bladder control.

Pregnancy and childbirth may affect the pelvic floor and the tissues supporting the bladder and urethra.

Menopause-related changes, constipation, chronic coughing, certain medications, neurologic conditions, pelvic organ prolapse, and diabetes can also contribute.

A urinary tract infection may temporarily increase urgency, frequency, burning, or leakage.

Daily habits may play a role as well.

Large amounts of caffeine or alcohol, severely restricting fluids, delaying bathroom trips, or ongoing constipation can affect symptoms for some people.

This does not mean you caused the problem or that changing one habit will resolve it.

It gives us more information to consider as we care for you.

### Where Vaginal Wellness Fits In

During menopause, declining estrogen can affect vaginal and urinary tissues.

This may contribute to dryness, burning, irritation, discomfort with sex, urgency, increased frequency, or repeated urinary tract symptoms.

Because these concerns can occur together, we describe this as vaginal wellness—our focus is your vaginal wellness as part of your overall health and comfort.

That does not mean every leak is hormone related—or that every woman needs hormone treatment.

Hormone therapy may be appropriate for some patients when symptoms and medical history support it, while other medications or nonhormonal treatments may be better for others.

A vaginal wellness procedure is not automatically the right answer either.

The goal is to understand the full picture before discussing options and to provide appropriate care whenever possible.

## What to Bring Up at a Consultation

You can start with, “I leak when I cough,” or “I’m tired of looking for a bathroom everywhere I go.”

Describe how often it happens, how much urine leaks, and whether you notice a trigger.

It may help to mention:

- Whether leakage occurs with coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting, exercise, or sex.
- Whether you experience sudden urgency or cannot reach the bathroom in time.
- How often you urinate during the day or wake up at night.
- Whether you have burning, pelvic pain, blood in your urine, recurrent urinary tract infections, or a weak stream.
- Whether you feel pressure, heaviness, or a bulging sensation in the vagina or pelvis.
- Whether you have pain with intimacy, vaginal dryness, or irritation.
- Whether you have difficulty starting urination or feel unable to empty your bladder.
- What medications, supplements, hormones, or medical conditions may be relevant.
- How symptoms affect exercise, travel, work, sleep, relationships, or your willingness to leave home.

Pelvic pressure, a bulging sensation, pain with intimacy, or difficulty emptying your bladder deserve assessment—not an assumption that your pelvic floor simply needs strengthening.

Pelvic floor exercises help some people, but the wrong exercises—or tightening muscles that are already overactive—may not improve every symptom.

## Everyday Steps That May Help

Care often starts with practical changes that may reduce symptoms and reveal patterns.

These steps may be combined with pelvic floor care, medication, hormone therapy, or other treatment when appropriate.

The best approach depends on the type and cause of your incontinence.

### Keep a Short Bladder Diary

For several days, record bathroom trips, leaks, drinks, urgency, and what you were doing when symptoms occurred.

This can help you and your provider identify patterns.

### Notice Your Triggers

Caffeine, alcohol, carbonated drinks, artificial sweeteners, and certain foods may worsen urgency or frequency for some people.

Look for your individual pattern rather than assuming you must give up everything you enjoy.

### Stay Appropriately Hydrated

Drastically cutting fluids can concentrate urine, worsen irritation, and contribute to constipation.

Fluid needs vary with your health, medications, and activity, so discuss them with your provider.

### Address Constipation and Ongoing Coughing

Straining and repeated pressure can contribute to leakage or pelvic floor symptoms.

Treating these issues may improve bladder control.

### Ask About Pelvic Floor and Bladder Training

Pelvic floor exercises may help some types of incontinence, while bladder training may help urgency and frequency.

Guidance can help you choose the right approach and avoid exercises that are not appropriate for you.

### Ask Whether Medication or Hormone Therapy May Help

Depending on the cause of your symptoms, prescription medication, local hormone therapy, or other medical treatment may be appropriate.

These options require an individualized review of your health history, medications, risks, and goals.

### Plan for Activities That Matter to You

Knowing where bathrooms are, using protective products, and adjusting timing around exercise or travel can help you stay active while exploring longer-term options.

Weight management may help some women when relevant to their health and goals.

It should be discussed supportively and individually—not as a requirement or judgment.

Depending on the diagnosis, medication, hormone therapy, a pessary, pelvic floor physical therapy, or specialist care may also be appropriate.

## Pads Should Not Be the Whole Conversation

Watch a football game and you may see a commercial for bladder leakage pads or protective underwear.

After enough of those messages, it can start to sound like leaking is simply what women should expect—and buying a more absorbent product is the answer.

Women deserve to hear about their choices.

**Pads can catch a leak, but they don’t tell you why it is happening or what might help reduce it.**

You should have the opportunity to understand the cause and explore care that fits your needs, whether that includes pelvic floor care, bladder training, medication, or another appropriate treatment.

There is no shame in using protection.

The problem is being left with the impression that protection is your only option.

Bladder leakage may be common, but “just wear a pad” should not be the end of the conversation.

## VTone, FormaV and Morpheus8V at A New You

At A New You Women’s Clinic in Springfield, we offer three treatments through the EmpowerRF system.

Here is what each one does and why we might discuss it with you.

### VTone Helps Exercise Your Pelvic Floor Muscles

Your pelvic floor is a group of muscles at the bottom of your pelvis.

Think of it as a hammock that helps support your bladder and other pelvic organs.

These muscles also help you control when urine comes out.

VTone uses a small applicator placed inside the vagina.

It sends electrical pulses that make the pelvic floor muscles squeeze and then relax.

This is called electrical muscle stimulation. Think of it as guided exercise for those muscles.

The goal is to help weak muscles work better and improve bladder control.

VTone may help some women with leaks when they cough or sneeze, leaks with a sudden urge, or both.

We first check whether this type of muscle training fits your needs.

Your plan may also include exercises to practice at home.

### FormaV Uses Warmth to Treat Vaginal Tissue

FormaV uses radiofrequency energy, or RF.

This is a type of energy that creates heat in tissue.

A smooth applicator delivers controlled warmth to vaginal tissue without needles, while the device tracks the temperature.

The warmth increases blood flow in the treated area.

It is also used to encourage collagen remodeling—changes in the fibers that help give tissue its shape and support.

Collagen is a protein that acts like a support net in your tissue.

Elastin is another protein that helps tissue stretch and spring back.

Both play a role in how firm and flexible tissue feels.

The aim of heating the tissue is to encourage changes in its support and firmness over time.

We may discuss FormaV when you have concerns about changes in vaginal tissue.

Whether those changes will improve your symptoms is something we discuss with you, not a result we can promise.

### Morpheus8V Combines Tiny Needles With Radiofrequency Heat

Morpheus8V uses microneedling—very small needles that make tiny, controlled openings in the tissue.

Those needles also deliver radiofrequency energy below the surface.

The small openings and heat trigger the body’s repair response.

That response can include making and reorganizing collagen, the protein that helps support tissue.

The goal is to change the firmness and structure of the treated tissue as it heals.

Because this treatment uses needles and heat, it needs more planning and healing time than a treatment that only warms the surface.

We explain comfort measures, aftercare, and when you can return to sex and other activities.

### Choosing Care That Fits You

VTone focuses on muscle function. FormaV and Morpheus8V focus on tissue treatment.

You may not need a device treatment at all, and you don’t need to choose one before your visit.

Research on vaginal radiofrequency treatments for bladder leakage and menopause symptoms is still limited.

**These treatments are not proven cures.**

Risks include burns, scarring, and pain, including pain with sex.

We review the possible benefits, risks, other choices, recovery, and cost with you before deciding on care.

## When to Seek Care Promptly

New burning with urination, blood in your urine, fever, chills, back or side pain, or significant pelvic pain needs prompt medical attention.

These symptoms may indicate an infection or another condition requiring evaluation.

**If you cannot urinate, seek urgent medical care.**

Severe pain, heavy bleeding, sudden weakness, or other rapidly worsening symptoms also require prompt evaluation.

Don’t wait for a routine vaginal wellness appointment.
